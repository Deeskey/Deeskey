Your Complete Guide to Vehicle Brakes

Your brakes are the single most important safety system on your vehicle, yet they’re easy to overlook until you hear a squeal or feel the steering wheel shake. Understanding how your brake system works—and catching wear early—saves you money and keeps you and your family safe on Utah County roads.

How Your Brake System Works

Every time you press the brake pedal, a team of hydraulic and mechanical components work together to bring thousands of pounds of metal to a smooth stop:

  • Brake Pads: The friction material that presses against the rotors to slow your wheels. Pads are designed to wear down over time and are the most commonly replaced brake component.
  • Brake Rotors: The metal discs attached to your wheel hubs. Over time, heat and friction can cause rotors to thin, score, or warp—leading to vibration when braking.
  • Calipers: The clamps that house the brake pads and use hydraulic pressure to squeeze them against the rotors. Sticking calipers can cause uneven wear and pulling.
  • Brake Fluid: The hydraulic fluid that transfers force from your foot on the pedal to the calipers. Brake fluid absorbs moisture over time, which lowers its boiling point and can cause corrosion inside the lines.
  • Anti-Lock Braking System (ABS): Sensors and a control module that prevent your wheels from locking up during hard stops, especially on wet or snowy Utah roads.

5 Warning Signs Your Brakes Need Attention

Don't wait until your brakes fail completely. Schedule an inspection if you notice any of these symptoms:

  • Squealing or Screeching: Most brake pads have a built-in metal wear indicator that squeals when the pad is getting thin.
  • Grinding Noise: A harsh metal-on-metal sound usually means the pad material is completely gone and the backing plate is chewing into your rotor.
  • Steering Wheel or Pedal Vibration: Pulsing when you brake is a classic sign of warped rotors or excessive rotor runout.
  • Soft or Spongy Pedal: If the pedal sinks closer to the floor than usual, you may have air in the brake lines, old fluid, or a hydraulic leak.
  • Pulling to One Side or Dashboard Lights: Pulling can point to a seized caliper, while an illuminated Brake or ABS light means the system needs a diagnostic scan.

How Often Should You Check Your Brakes?

Depending on your driving habits—like stop-and-go city traffic, towing, or driving down canyon roads—brake pads typically last between 30,000 and 60,000 miles. Having your pads, rotors, calipers, and fluid inspected during routine oil changes is the easiest way to catch wear before it turns into a more expensive rotor or caliper replacement.
